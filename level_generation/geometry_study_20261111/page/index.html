How much more geometry should the buildings get?

Engine captures from five of your eleven poses, each next to two Codex image edits: A, a modest modular upgrade, and B, a hand-modelled hero version. The edits show what the extra geometry would look like. They are not renders.

Aim for A. Give walls real thickness at windows and doors, raise the timber off the plaster, give roofs slab thickness, overhangs and ridge caps, chamfer corners and posts, and add a plinth and coping stones. That is about 1,500 to 4,000 triangles per building, all within the same textures. Keep B for a few landmarks (the castle, temple, tower), if anywhere.

Most of the gain in these images comes from edges and silhouettes: roofs that end in a shadowed overhang instead of a paper edge, and windows that sit in the wall. Parallax and the surface maps already handle the relief inside each face. What they cannot do is change an outline. That is the part geometry has to add.

Shelved on 2026-11-12. The kit applied to all of New Sorpigal still collided with what is painted and modelled on the buildings:

  • striped textures on the porch posts;
  • door frames over carved friezes;
  • corner stones that read as loose blocks;
  • knee braces that meet neither post nor beam;
  • window frames that miss the painted frames.

The project is kept as an experiment. Its handoff (level_generation/projects/mm6_sorpigal_geometry_pilot/HANDOFF.md) lists what was tried, the open issues with screenshots, and how to resume.

Silhouette kit prototype

Your note on the Free Haven stable was right. Most of the automatic pass's triangles went into relief taken from the painted textures: timbers raised 5–6 units, windows set in by 12. That can't be seen from normal distance, and the window recesses baked black. What the concepts add is a short list of large pieces, and the kit builds exactly those:

  • the eave continued outward in the roof's own plane, with a soffit, a heavy fascia board and rafter ends (no lip over a sign or a door under the eave);
  • corner posts on timber walls, and corner stones on stone walls;
  • door frames with a threshold step;
  • full window frames (jambs, head and sill), proud of the wall;
  • openings set in by only 4–6 units, and no raised painted timber.

On six buildings across the five towns the kit goes from 20,863 to 12,214 triangles (−41%). The roof edge is the part that reads most clearly: every roof gets a real overhang and a shadow line. Door frames and corner posts show at street level. Flat-roofed buildings, such as the Silver Cove tavern, gain the least.

Fixed after your review of the prototype:

  • The lip used to stop either side of a door, which left pieces that read as separate porches. It now runs all the way round. On a roof with a blade sign under its eave (the stable's horse sign), the whole roof's lip is made shorter and its fascia slimmer, with no rafter ends, until no new face cuts into the sign's box (an exact test in the generator). The sign stays where it was. A notch in the rim, lowering the sign and hanging it from an arm were all tried: moving the sign opened a hole in the wall it was set into.
  • On timber houses the soffit and fascia are now in the house's own timber; grey slate boards looked like a different material.
  • Door frames now stop below the eave; lintels used to stick up into the roof.
  • Hip caps now run out to the lip corners, so they no longer end as stubs on the old roof corner.
  • Timber houses get wooden steps instead of grey stone ones.

Round 2: towards Concept A

After your review, round 2 changes the rules, not just the examples you pointed at. Every view below has four states: the concept, the map today, round 1 and round 2. Round 2 is engine captures with a 64-sample bake.

  • Trim matches what it sits on. In round 1, one light granite trim went everywhere. Round 2 has eight trim textures generated with Codex, each a dressed or board version of its host's texture: the town rubble, Goblinwatch's black stone, the tower's sandstone, the temple's marble, terracotta for the brick buildings, barn-red boards, and cedar, grey and slate caps for the three roof families. Caps on the grey slate roofs are now slate, not red wood.
  • Size and outline as in the concept. Coping is thicker and overhangs, with a weathered top. The wall piers have pyramid caps. The gate keeps its own rubble tops and gets a low stepped base. Goblinwatch has no battlements (the concept has none), a slimmer plinth, a lighter cornice on corbels, and no stripes of quoins on the towers.
  • Everything is supported. Post bases are clipped to the floor they stand on, so the tavern's corner bases end flush with the platform. Windows get one sill each (round 1 put a bar under every pane of the barn windows), and roof caps stop at slope breaks (the temple roof's red stripes).
  • Tavern: short, thick knee braces, solid post shoes, mouldings and joist ends on the porch roof, a brick nosing on the platform, stone surrounds round the windows, and a stone course at the wall foot.
  • Dock and ship: a new deck texture with broad golden planks laid across the pier, raised edge strips and sparse piles. On the ship: heavier yards with collars on the mast, cap rails on the bulwarks, and framed gun ports.

How it is checked now: each view goes to Codex (gpt-6-astra) as concept, original and result, with the rules above and none of our notes, and comes back as a list of differences. I check each one against crops and fix it, then the town is rebuilt and checked again (five rounds so far). The reviewer is strict and still lists differences on every view. These are now mostly how strongly features read: window hoods, door thresholds and steps, bargeboards on gables, and the ship's gun ports. The plinth under half-timbering stays out, as you asked, though the concepts show it.

Lighting: the bake gives every new face its own lightmap chart. That cannot fit in the original 17.2 MB at any texel density, and today's file is 79.4 MB. The research (LIGHTING_PROPOSAL.md) recommends that detail faces borrow the lightmap chart of the face they replace or sit on. The small shadows (recesses, sills, cap undersides) would come from the runtime sun shadow maps plus one baked occlusion value per vertex. Estimated file: about 17.6 MB, about 15.4 MB with tighter padding. This needs an engine change (loader, shader, a sun-shadow caster pass for BModels), opt-in per map.

What these maps needed that New Sorpigal didn't:

  • Class and height maps. Relief reads each texture's surface-material maps, and 44–93% of these maps' wall area had none. The existing surface-material pipeline (mat.py auto, two Codex calls per texture) made about 200 of them. They are installed in assets_source and not cooked into the game, so the comparisons here are about geometry only.
  • A relief budget. Snowy logs, rubble and noisy class maps first broke faces into thousands of small pieces: 1.26 million triangles on Frozen Highlands. Now any face whose relief gets too complex stays flat, and plain beams, boards and logs get no raised wood. Every building is now under about 8,000 triangles.
  • New automatic rules, also applied to New Sorpigal (round 3):
    • wood-on-wood walls take their frames from the height map;
    • coping goes only on real wall tops (stair treads got it), slim on ashlar and chunky on rubble;
    • rafter ends go only where an eave really overhangs a wall;
    • doors get jambs and a lintel, and flat-topped windows get a head;
    • windows get one sill per window, not per pane;
    • log and plank houses get a stone footing (never painted half-timbering);
    • a plinth on the ground gets no bottom face (one had baked a black patch round Free Haven's dungeon entrance).

Astra still lists differences on every view. Across Frozen Highlands, Castle Ironfist and Free Haven the most frequent are, in order: eaves and gable edges (verge boards), door steps and thresholds, dressed corner stones, window surrounds, plinths, then coping and rafter ends. These are the next round. Frozen Highlands' round balcony parapet is capped only in part. The lighting files grow 3–6 times because each new face gets its own lightmap chart; the lighting change would bring them back to their original size.

What A is made of

Walls and openings

  • Windows and doors recessed 10–20 units, with frame, sill and lintel standing proud
  • Half-timber beams and corner boards raised a few units off the plaster
  • Stone plinth or foundation course along the base
  • Chamfered wall corners

Roofs

  • A thick slab instead of a single face
  • Eaves overhang with fascia and visible rafter ends
  • Ridge caps, and barge boards on the gables
  • Straight silhouette: shingles stay in the texture and parallax

Wood, stone, bridges

  • Posts with chamfers, bases and simple brackets
  • Steps with real treads and nosing
  • Coping stones along bridge parapets and town walls
  • Arch voussoirs on bridges and gates

How it would be built

Most of A can be generated from data we already have, and then touched up by hand per building. None of it has to be modelled from scratch.

  • Where the windows are. Windows, doors and timbers are painted into the textures, not modelled. The surface material maps already mark each texel's class (glass, wood, iron, plaster…) and height, so a script can find the window and door rectangles in a face's UVs, cut them and recess them. It can also turn the wood strips on plaster into raised beams.
  • Roofs and edges. Roof faces are easy to tell apart by texture and slope. A generator thickens them, extends the eaves and adds ridge and fascia strips. A bevel pass handles corners and posts.
  • Textures. The new mesh reuses the 4× masters with the same UVs on the original faces. New strips (fascia, sills, coping) take a small shared trim sheet in the same art style.
  • In the engine. A BModel gets a visual mesh drawn in place of its faces. The original faces stay, invisible, for collision, door clicks, events and picking, so gameplay does not change. The far LOD can be the original faces themselves. The lighting bake must be redone for every changed map.
  • Dungeons. The same approach works for BLV maps (trims at floor and ceiling edges, door and arch frames, pillars), but the faces are seen from inside. Detail has to stay inside its sector and clear of portals and the party's collision. That makes it a second phase, after the town pilot.

What these images exaggerate

  • Codex also lightly cleaned and relit some surfaces. The real version keeps today's textures and lighting, plus the shadows of the new geometry once the bake is redone.
  • B's ragged roof edges need each tile row modelled and UV-mapped to the shingle texture. That is the expensive part of B, and in-game it mostly shows on the silhouette against the sky.
  • The castle roof texture in B changed from a noisy pattern to real slates. That texture needs replacing anyway, whatever we do with the geometry.

What the pilot showed

  • About 1,000–2,500 triangles per building, mostly automatic. The hand-written part was a few rectangles (doors, painted pilasters), post bracket directions, the face lists for roofs, coping and arches, and a few depth values per texture family.
  • No engine change was needed. The only tool change is an opt-in finer lightmap density for the detail models (4 units per texel instead of 32). That density is what lets the small recesses shade at all. Its cost is the main open question: with these seven BModels the map's lighting file goes from 17.2 MB to 36.4 MB (both baked with today's tool). A whole town needs a coarser density (8 or 16) or a shared atlas.
  • Dark materials need contrast and big forms. Two new seamless textures (dark basalt ashlar for Goblinwatch, light granite ashlar for the bridge and the Armory plinth) were generated with Codex, cooked into the stage and baked, without touching the installed assets.
  • One bug found and fixed on the way: steps along a wall's bottom edge lay on the terrain and baked black patches into it. Those faces are now skipped.
  • At this sun angle the gain is real but quiet: silhouettes, eaves and porch posts change the most, and flat timbers the least. Ambient occlusion and lower sun angles would bring out more.
  • Not done yet: plinths along the bases, a stone plinth under the keep, and the seams where new faces meet.
