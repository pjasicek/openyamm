Design proposal

OpenYAMM Map Studio

A level is designed in a known order: plan the place, shape the space, build it, wire its mechanisms, populate it, dress it, and play it. The studio follows that order. This revision rebuilds the design from that process, from how existing editors and the original designers worked, and from five original maps that every workflow below has to be able to rebuild.

Stages, not data types

Revision 1 grouped tools by what they edit (terrain, models, spawns). Now the top bar is the design process. Each stage shows its tools and a checklist of what is still open.

A plan view and a 3D view of one map

Rooms are drawn in a top-down plan and checked in 3D, like Doom Builder and Mapster32 on the same sector-and-portal model BLV uses. Outdoors, the plan is the map seen from above.

Mechanisms you can see

Doors, levers, plates, keys and teleports are presets drawn as connections. Goblinwatch's 16-key vault becomes one switch board with a simulator, instead of 16 hand-written handlers.

Research

What level designers and editors teach

Each finding comes with what it means for the studio. Sources are linked.

Design happens in stages

The Level Design Book's order: pre-production, layout (thumbnails, bubble diagram, floor plan with the critical path), blockout, scripting, lighting, art, release. An art pass made too early locks in layout mistakes. A blockout is tested by walking it, not flying: “99% of the time, your blockout will not survive a playtest.”

leveldesignbook.com/process · /layout · /blockout

Studio: seven stages on the top bar; the plan stays attached to the map; walk mode at party size from the first room.

Draw rooms in 2D, check them in 3D

Doom Builder and Mapster32 draw a closed polygon to make a sector, draw across a shared edge to join two, and raise floors and ceilings by aiming in 3D. One data model, two synchronised views. Mapster32 copies a surface with Tab, pastes it with Enter, and plays from the camera with Ctrl+P.

Mapster32 keys · Doomworld

Studio: BLV is the same sector-and-portal model. Rooms are drawn; portals and sectors are derived.

Wiring must be visible and checked

Hammer connects an output (OnPressed) to a target's input with a delay and a count; an Inputs tab shows what drives an object, and broken links turn red in Check for Problems. Legend of Grimrock draws connectors as lines from plates and buttons to doors, and tests the dungeon inside the editor.

Valve I/O · Grimrock editor

Studio: Connections tab on every object, lines in both views, issues with quick fixes.

Roads shape the ground

Unreal's landscape splines carry a width and falloff, raise or lower the terrain and paint a ground layer, on their own edit layer so later sculpting does not break them. Neverwinter Nights paints roads and rivers as “crossers” over tiles; Heroes 3 and Tiled pick transition tiles automatically.

UE splines · Tiled terrain sets

Studio: road and river splines that grade the 128 × 128 grid and choose MM road, diagonal and shore tiles from the bank.

Kits need standard sizes

Bethesda built over 400 Skyrim dungeon cells from 7 kits. Footprints are multiples of each other, there is one door-frame size, pivots sit on the floor centre, and “snap to reference” places pieces at any angle.

Burgess, GDC 2013

Studio: building and room kits from MM6–MM9 with fixed door metrics and snap to plot, road or wall.

Locks and keys form a graph

Mark Brown's Boss Keys diagrams draw rooms, keys and locks together. Jennell Jaquays' dungeons loop: several entrances, shortcuts and secret paths, so the party can circle back. Hard locks must be solved; soft locks can be bypassed at a cost.

Jaquaying the dungeon · Lock and key dungeons

Studio: the flow graph is generated from the map itself and checks the critical path.

Landmarks pull the player

Breath of the Wild uses three scales of landform and triangles that hide a landmark's base until you approach. The Witcher 3 team wanted two points of interest on the horizon at all times. Disney calls them weenies.

BotW at CEDEC 2017 · Witcher 3

Studio: a landmark tool that shows where on the map it can be seen.

What made editors clunky

Unreal 4 users waited minutes per undo on brush levels. OpenMW-CS shows two huge tables instead of navigation and lacks transform keys. Divinity: Original Sin 2's tools were called complicated and crash-prone. Doom Builder's auto-finish option confused people. Hammer++ became loved for autosaving open maps on a crash.

UE forum · OpenMW forum · PC Gamer

Studio: no modal dialogs, unlimited undo, autosave, one mode at a time with its name always visible.

The original games

How NWC and the modders built these maps

Nobody from New World Computing described the in-house editor. The credits, a few interviews and the shipped data still show how the maps were made, and the community tools show what hurts.

Two engines, many hands

MM6 had an outdoor engine called Horizon and an indoor engine called Labyrinth. Ten people are credited with level design on MM6 and four on MM8 (lead Richard Corredera). According to Tim Lang, some MM6 dungeons came from a separate team in Wisconsin, and the Tomb of VARN was modelled in LightWave and adapted to Labyrinth.

MM6 pre-release FAQ · Lang interview summary · credits

Studio: geometry from outside tools has to be first-class, not an afterthought.

Towns from prefab buildings

Shadowspire's models are named 06n_poor1N, 06n_med3E, 06n_rich2N, 06n_temple_AN…DN: a regional library in rich, medium and poor tiers, big buildings in parts A–D, and a facing letter in every name. Every MM8 outdoor file still carries the header blank / default.odm: maps were cloned from one template.

checked in the shipped data

Studio: a kit library with tiers, multi-part assemblies and facings is enough to build an MM town.

Scripts are short and repetitive

Across every MM6–MM8 map script: 5 263 SetDoorState, 2 883 OpenChest, 1 565 EnterHouse, 1 007 StatusText, 556 MoveToMap, 448 SetTexture, 389 SummonMonsters, 366 CastSpell. Most events are one or two lines with a hover hint.

counted in assets_dev/worlds/mm6–mm8/events/maps

Studio: one-click behaviour presets cover most events; Lua stays for the rest.

What modders fought with

GrayFace's MMEditor (in MMExtension) is an in-game editor: Alt+F1, geometry imported as OBJ, rooms and portals marked with _Portal_ textures, spawns as objects named m1–m3. Modders report missing documentation, that changing any vertex wipes the properties of its faces “even door settings”, and that experienced people import a whole dungeon as one room to avoid portals.

MMEditor readme · “Modelling problems” thread

Studio: derive portals; keep face and door properties through edits and re-imports; help inside every tool.

The workflow

Seven stages

The stage bar is the spine of the studio. A stage picks the default view, the tool rail and the checklist; it never locks anything, and any stage is one key away (1–7). Most people go round the loop several times: plan, shape, test, back to the plan.

Ten principles

Follow the design order, never lock it

Stages filter tools and show what is open; they do not stop anyone jumping ahead.

The plan is the checklist

Planned rooms, buildings and points of interest stay on the map as ghost outlines until something real takes their place.

Rooms, not polygon soup

Draw rooms; sectors, portals and the BSP are derived on save. Nobody marks a portal by hand.

Presets first, Lua second

Door, lever, chest, house, exit, trap, fountain and the rest are presets that write readable Lua. Any preset can be turned into a script.

Connections are visible

Selecting a lever draws lines to its doors. Every object lists what drives it and what it drives.

Imported geometry keeps its properties

Faces have stable identities. Moving vertices or re-importing a mesh keeps textures, flags, events and doors; anything that cannot be matched is listed.

Test in under a second

Walk at party size inside the studio; Play here starts OpenYAMM at the camera with test switches and comes back to the same view.

Validation is always on

Unreachable rooms, keys behind their own lock, events with no target, overlapping faces. Each issue jumps to the object and offers a fix.

Tool options and object properties are separate

The bar above the views holds the active tool's options. The inspector holds the selection's properties. Nothing opens in a modal dialog.

Help where you work

Every tool has a short explanation and an example from an original map (“as Goblinwatch's vault”). Missing documentation was the loudest MMEditor complaint.

Who makes what

Made in the studio, or imported

The studio makes everything that carries game rules: heights, rooms, doors, clickable faces and placement. Outside tools make what is only shape and art. The studio is not a modelling tool.

Buildings stay engine geometry

Outdoor buildings are BModels: faces with their own textures, collision, click events and baked light. An imported GLB becomes BModel faces, and its material names become texture names. The kit library holds the MM6–MM9 buildings as prefabs in tiers and multi-part assemblies, the way NWC built its towns.

Re-import keeps every setting

Every face keeps a stable identity. When a building is imported again, faces are matched by position, normal and texture, and their doors, events and flags carry over. Faces that cannot be matched are listed, never silently cleared.

Dungeons: drawn shell, imported detail

The shell is drawn. An imported mesh is attached to the room it stands in and becomes that room's faces. Importing a whole dungeon and splitting it into rooms automatically can come later; it is not the main route.

Doors: library or selection

Library doors come with geometry, texture, motion, speed and sound: portcullis, wooden door, double sliding door, secret wall, sinking pillar, lift platform, cell door, hinged door. Dropped into an opening, one sizes itself. For unique cases, select faces (a bridge deck, a floor, a wall section) and make them a door. Both are painted like any other face.

What the studio does not do

Vertex editing stops at room corners, door shapes and nudging faces. No mesh sculpting, no UV unwrapping beyond aligning textures on faces, no creatures. Those stay in Blender and the existing model pipeline.

Importing a building, and importing it again

Re-import keeps the door. The temple came back from Blender with a porch. 210 faces matched and kept their settings; the 4 new porch faces are listed; one material whose name is not a texture is flagged.

Doors: from the library, or from a selection

Most doors come from the library. Dropped into an opening, the portcullis fits itself and the inspector shows what drives it. Unique doors, like Gharik's sliding bridge, start from a selection instead.

Step 2 · Plan: zones, road sketch and points of interest

The plan becomes the checklist. Nothing here is geometry yet. The map card carries what MM keeps outside the map file: neighbours, travel days and the three monster slots.

Steps 5–6 · Build and wire: plots, kit buildings and the service picker

Buildings snap to plots and face the road. The door distance is measured from the real transformed door, the rule from your frontage calibration. Picking the service is a field on the door, not a script.

  • Coast stamp from a zone outline; sea level; shore tiles chosen automatically
  • Road splines that grade terrain and pick MM bank tiles, including diagonals
  • Bridge suggestion where a road crosses water
  • Plots with frontage; kit or imported buildings that face the road; graded approaches
  • Multi-part assemblies kept in one frame
  • Service picker over the house table; bulk residences
  • Presets: well, fountain, obelisk, shrine, skill check, consumable tree, ambush plate, timer
  • Scatter with keep-out rules for roads, plots and doorways

Architecture

One core, three front ends

The core is one C++ library built twice: to WebAssembly for the browser and the agent server, and natively for the command-line tool and CI. The browser editor, agents and CI run the same operations, compiler and checks. A map an agent built opens in the editor exactly as the agent left it, and the other way round.

Browser editor

The hosted static page for players, or the same build served locally by oymap studio when you work with agents.

Agents

Claude Code, Codex or any MCP client, through the MCP server or the oymap command line.

CI

Golden projects compiled and checked on every core change; headless playtests of exported maps.

Rules for the interface

The command line first, MCP on top

oymap … --json is the source of truth and works the same in Claude Code, Codex and CI. The MCP server is a thin layer over it.

Readable ids, never indices

room:vault, door:d36, evt:lever_gate. The compiler assigns the engine's numbers; agents never see face 4412 unless they ask.

Relations, not coordinates

Rooms have anchors (sides, corners, centre, sockets). Doors connect two anchors; spawns are zones with counts; terrain edits run between anchors. The core snaps to the grid. Raw coordinates are an override, not the default.

Graph before geometry

Rooms, locks and keys are checked as a graph first, then laid out, then dressed. Each step has its own checks, so structure is fixed before walls.

Every edit is a transaction

An edit returns a diff, a check delta and a new revision. dry_run previews; checkpoints and revert are cheap because the project is text in git.

Errors say how to fix it

Every error names the object, the rule, the valid range and a corrected example operation, never an error code alone.

Concise by default

Results stay near 2 000 tokens. Details come by paging or by link to a file, under Claude Code's result limits and Codex's 60-second tool timeout.

Scale is checked, not trusted

Party size from world.yml, door, corridor and step standards from the calibration maps. A tree the size of a mountain is a check failure, not a surprise in a screenshot.

Pictures are labelled and repeatable

Fixed camera sets, ids drawn on every object, one contact sheet, the previous revision beside it. Agents point at labels instead of guessing positions.

Code for bulk work

map_exec runs loops over many rooms or maps in one call. Scripts that prove useful are promoted into the core library, which replaces per-project helper scripts over time.

Handles instead of sessions

The newest MCP revision has no sessions, so every call carries doc@revision. A write against an old revision fails with a note of what you changed meanwhile, and the agent re-reads.

Long work is a job

Compiles, renders and playtests run as MCP tasks where the client supports them, with job_start and job_status for the rest, and progress messages throughout.

Assets: the library first, art made outside

Search before making

map_assets searches what exists: installed decorations, trees and props, building and door kits, the 4× wall textures and surface materials, the 3D creatures. Something new is made only when nothing fits.

Production stays in the pipelines

Blender for modelling, Codex image generation for textures and surface materials, Meshy with its texturing for heavier props, tools/install_model.py to install. The studio never generates art.

Placeholders with a brief

When a map needs something that does not exist, the agent places a sized placeholder with a short brief: what it is, its footprint, a reference image, the world. The map stays playable and checkable meanwhile.

Swap keeps the settings

Once the asset is installed, the placeholder becomes the real model at the same place, with its events and flags. The open placeholders are the art queue.

Roadmap

Milestones, tested by agents and by hand

The full plan is in the repository at devdocs/map_studio/PLAN.md, with the order for retiring the old editor in EDITOR_RETIREMENT.md. The core comes first and replaces the old editor's build steps; the browser editor is built on a working core.

M0 · L

Core extraction, old editor retired

New core/ library; formats, scene parsers, tables and terrain moved out of game/ and engine/; the indoor compiler and the outdoor build moved out of editor/; oymap build-map replaces the editor's headless build; editor/ deleted.

Accepted when: the game is unchanged and every BLV and ODM project rebuilds byte-identical through the new commands.

M1 · L

Project format and the read side

Complete scene writers, import of every MM6–MM9 map into projects, stable ids, checks, flow graph, labelled renders, the oymap read commands.

Accepted when: all maps import and round-trip, and the audit finds the issues listed in the walkthroughs.

M2 · L

Operations, host and agent interface

Wire, Populate and Dress operations, overlay compile, the local host, the MCP server, code mode, asset catalogue and placeholders, the game's dev mount and reload.

Accepted when: an agent re-wires Goblinwatch's vault and New Sorpigal's wells as an overlay that plays the same.

M3 · XL

Browser editor: view and review

Plan and 3D views, walk mode, flow graph, pins, live pairing, the hosted build.

Accepted when: you walk all five maps, pin one, and an agent resolves the pins live.

M4 · XL

Indoor authoring

Rooms, caves, stairs, doors from the library or from a selection, lifts, liquids, themes, set-piece import, the Wire stage UI.

Accepted when: Goblinwatch, the Necromancer's Lab and Gharik's Forge are rebuilt from nothing, by an agent and by hand.

M5 · XL

Outdoor authoring

Zones, landform and heightmap import, road splines with MM tiles, plots, kit and imported buildings, scatter, landmarks.

Accepted when: New Sorpigal and Shadowspire are rebuilt, and the first MM5 region is built on the core.

M6 · L

MM9 profiles and sharing

Open-air regions and cities, water volumes, ladders, destructibles, cinematics, the viewer inside the chat, named mods.
